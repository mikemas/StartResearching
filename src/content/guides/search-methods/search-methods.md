  <p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Genealogy research can be summarized as:</p><ol data-rte-list="default"><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"> Finding relevant, authentic sources</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Analyzing those sources for information that leads to evidence</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Assembling all the evidence from a variety of sources into a complete picture of a person’s life, with proof of their relationships.</p></li></ol><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">When we talk about search methods we’re not just talking about the first stage of finding sources, but also about the second stage in which we analyze those sources.  In the examples discussed below we look at our records for names of individuals that may be in close association with our ancestor or research subject, and then we conduct searches for those people, with the hopes of finding new records related to our ancestors. </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">&nbsp;</p><h3 style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><strong>“FAN Club”, Cluster Research, and Collateral Research</strong></h3><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">These three terms, while similar and often interchanged, have slightly different meanings. They all involve researching individuals whose lives surround our ancestor or research subject, but may or may not be related, or may not even cross paths during their lifetimes.&nbsp;</p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Noted genealogist Elizabeth Shown Mills, CG, CGL, came up with the term <strong>“FAN club”</strong>, short for a group of <strong>F</strong>riends, <strong>A</strong>ssociates, and <strong>N</strong>eighbors (though I’ve also seen it written as Friends &amp; Family, Associates, and Neighbors) who could be thought of as an individual's fan club.&nbsp;</p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><strong>Cluster research</strong> is a broader term for researching the community members or specific group of people whose lives are near that of our ancestors. In my view, the FAN club principle is a type of cluster research.&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">A few examples of people you might research with the FAN club principle or cluster research::&nbsp;</p><ul data-rte-list="default"><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Neighbors shown to live close on a census record, or that own land nearby shown in town histories or land surveys</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">People with the same surname on a census record, or in a list of church members, or in a military unit, and so forth</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Witness names on a baptism record, marriage record, naturalization record, etc.&nbsp;</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Names listed on an obituary or shown in a will or probate document, and informants on death certificates</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Ship passengers with the same surname or from the same town&nbsp;</p></li></ul><p class="" data-rte-preserve-empty="true" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">While <strong>collateral research</strong> is similar to FAN club and cluster research, it refers specifically to examining collateral relatives, that is ancestors linked to your bloodline by marriage or extended family, with the hope their stories might offer clues on your direct ancestor or research subject. With collateral research, you might look for marriage records of your ancestor’s in-laws, or census records of a child’s aunt, or birth records of your ancestors’ siblings.&nbsp;</p><p class="" data-rte-preserve-empty="true" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">By identifying these individuals whose lives intersect or run parallel with our ancestors, we can do things like:&nbsp;</p><ul data-rte-list="default"><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Link a source to our ancestor, especially when a record is a common name.&nbsp;</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Discover information about a person’s parents by looking at the records of their siblings</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Follow migratory patterns of a group of people, and possibly find where our ancestors moved</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Find additional sources that may not have come up before.</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Trace female ancestors by their male associates, especially since no records of females may exist in some time periods.</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Understand how our ancestors lived by learning about the lives of those from the same background and economic class or with shared beliefs or other things in common</p></li></ul><p class="" data-rte-preserve-empty="true" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"></p><h3 style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><strong>Additional Search Methods</strong></h3><ul data-rte-list="default"><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><strong>Whole-family genealogy</strong>: researching everyone in the family, not just direct descendants. Usually begins with a progenitor, like the first immigrant to reach America, and includes all of that person’s descendants </p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><strong>Surname studies</strong>: researching everyone with the same surname, sometimes limited to a certain time and place. May show variants of surnames, and history. Many surname studies already exist and may be helpful to search.</p><ul data-rte-list="default"><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><a href="https://one-name.org/">Guild of One-Name Studies</a></p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><a href="https://surname-society.org/">The Surname Society</a></p></li></ul></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><strong>Location studies</strong>: researching the entire population of a small town or village. </p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><strong>DNA searching:</strong> establishing relationships based on genetics. See <a href="/dna-testing">DNA Testing</a></p></li></ul><p class="" data-rte-preserve-empty="true" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Every genealogist uses their own tried-and-true combination of strategies, which may or may not include these. We encourage you to explore various methods and figure out which styles of research work best for you.&nbsp; </p><p class="" data-rte-preserve-empty="true" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">See also: <a href="/10-steps">10 Steps to Start Researching</a>; <a href="/search-fields">Search Fields</a> and  <a href="/correlating-evidence">Correlating Evidence</a></p><p class="" data-rte-preserve-empty="true" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"></p><p class="" data-rte-preserve-empty="true" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"></p><h3 style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><strong>Additional References:</strong></h3><ul data-rte-list="default"><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Elizabeth Shown Mills, “QuickLesson 11: Identity Problems &amp; the FAN Principle,”&nbsp;<em>Evidence Explained: Historical Analysis, Citation &amp; Source Usage </em>(<a href="https://www.evidenceexplained.com/content/quicklesson-11-identity-problems-fan-principle" target="_blank">https://www.evidenceexplained.com/content/quicklesson-11-identity-problems-fan-principle</a> : [Accessed 18 August 2020]).</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Elizabeth Shown Mills, <a href="http://tinyurl.com/7mltmyz"><em>QuickSheet: The Historical Biographer’s Guide to Cluster Research (the FAN Principle)</em> </a> Baltimore: GPC, 2012.</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Christina Cowan, “FAN Club Research to Build Your Family Tree”,<em> </em>Ancestry, <em>YouTube </em>(<a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=guKiQOK5Rc4:">https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=guKiQOK5Rc4:</a> [Accessed 18 August 2020])</p></li></ul>
