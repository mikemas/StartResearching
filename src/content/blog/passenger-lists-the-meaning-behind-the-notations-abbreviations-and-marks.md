<div class="sqs-html-content" data-sqsp-text-block-content>
  <p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">When you look at a list of passengers arriving at a port in the U.S., you’ll see the names of those traveling on the ship, as well as their ages, the port and country they departed from, and perhaps their place of birth. It might show how much money they arrived with in their pockets. They may have given a name and address of their connection in the country (which weren’t always factual), if they are able to read and write in English, their race, if they are married or single, their occupation, and their intended destination. That’s a lot of valuable information for a genealogist.  </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">However, if you’re not taking a look at the notations, abbreviations, and marks on the page you might be missing even more. Perhaps the person listed on the manifest never even made it on board the ship! </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">In this post we’ll go over some of the more prolific abbreviations and markings, including: </p><ul data-rte-list="default"><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Ditto or Quotation Marks</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">USB (U.S. Born) or USC (U.S. Citizen)</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">S.I. (Special Inquiry) and “X” and Several Reasons for Being Held (Husb., Bro, LPC, Under 16, Tel, Tel $, Med, DCD, LCD, No Boat) </p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Line Through Names</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Not on the Ship (DNS or Did Not Sail; NOB or Not On Board; NS or Not Shipped) </p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Naturalization Numbers</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">DEP</p></li></ul><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">While there were some discrepancies between ports and variations between years, you should be able to determine the meaning behind the markings in this post.  You should also check out <a href="https://www.jewishgen.org/infofiles/manifests/" target="_blank">“A Guide to Interpreting Passenger List Annotations” by Marian Smith, available on JewishGen.org</a>, for a more in-depth, qualified analysis of these and other notations and markings.  Marian L. Smith, though recently retired, was the senior historian at U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS), previously Immigration and Naturalization Service (INS), having joined the later in 1988 as their first historian (SHFG.org).  </p><p class="" data-rte-preserve-empty="true" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"></p><h3 style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Ditto or Quotation Marks</h3><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Let’s start with an easy one that you’ve likely come across before in your work - things that mean “ditto” or “same as above”. This could be a quotation mark (or typed as _”_  or double quotation marks, ), the word “ditto”,  or ditto abbreviated as “do”, or another letter or symbol (like the letter “V”). In the image below, what do you see?</p>

  <h3 style="white-space:pre-wrap;">U.S.B. or U.S.C</h3><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">In addition to “U.S. Born” (shown in image above) you may see “U.S.B.”. Also “U.S.C” for United States Citizen (See Smith’s section on <a href="https://www.jewishgen.org/infofiles/manifests/natlty/" target="_blank">“Annotations Regarding Nationality and Citizenship”</a>). </p><h3 style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><br>Special Inquiry or “X” </h3><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">A<strong> Special Inquiry </strong>meant cause for further examination, usually in the form of more interviews by officials or doctors.<strong> </strong>May show as the initials <strong>“S.I.”</strong> or <strong>“B.S.I.”</strong> for<strong> Board of Special Inquiry</strong>. May be a stamp of <strong>“Admitted”</strong> or <strong>“Deported”</strong> on or above the initials.  Appears in the left column before the name. </p>

<div class="sqs-html-content" data-sqsp-text-block-content>
  <p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"> </p><h3 style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Several reasons an alien would be held for Special Inquiry or Detained: </h3><ul data-rte-list="default"><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Women (or children) waiting for someone to meet them. You may see <strong>“Husb”</strong> or <strong>“Bro”</strong> indicating their husband or brother was on their way to pick them up, as women were generally not allowed to travel alone. These passengers were likely detained for a short time.</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><strong>“LPC”</strong> or Likely Public Charge, or those likely to become a burden to tax payers. Many reasons for LPC like being a single woman, not having enough money, or having a medical condition. Could be grounds for deportation. </p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Similarly <strong>“Under 16”</strong> was indicative of minor children not traveling to reunite with a parent.</p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><strong>“Tel”</strong> or <strong>“Tel $”</strong> meaning Telegram, meaning they didn’t have sufficient funds to travel to their intended destination, and they sent a telegram requesting money from a relative or someone else. </p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><strong>“MED”</strong> or medical reasons. These people may have been sent to hospitals or local medical buildings (some ports like Ellis Island had their own) or in some cases deported. You may also see <strong>D.C.D</strong> or <strong>L.C.D.</strong> for <strong>Loathsome or</strong> <strong>Dangerous Contagious Disease</strong>. </p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><strong>“No boat”</strong>, meaning a smaller boat wasn’t available to take them to shore or the dock just yet, and they had to wait. </p></li><li><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><strong>“STOW”</strong>, a stowaway, usually deported.  </p></li></ul><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">This list above is not complete. For example, an immigrant may have been rejected for being a criminal or anarchist. Explore more reasons on Smith’s “<a href="https://www.jewishgen.org/infofiles/manifests/bsi/causes.html" target="_blank">Causes (Grounds) for Exclusion Noted on BSI Lists, ca. 1903-1924”</a>. </p>

  <h3 style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Naturalization Numbers</h3><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Some notations could even be done many years later when an alien applied for a passport or citizenship. While a record of immigration wasn’t required for naturalization until 1906, notations were not generally made until 1926 to cut down on fraud (Smith). Annotating these passenger lists helped prevent other people from using the same name and information when applying to be a citizen. </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"> These <strong>naturalization numbers</strong> may be written by the passenger’s name, but are more likely to the right under nationality. There may be a date, either referring to the date the information was verified, or the date of naturalization. You may also see “NAT” or a similar abbreviation before the number. </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">The number itself may refer to the number on the passenger’s naturalization certificate. Other numbers written adjacent may refer to the location of the court where the person was naturalized. These numbers can sometimes be used to help track down your ancestor’s naturalization papers (see <a href="https://www.jewishgen.org/infofiles/manifests/occ/" target="_blank">Marian Smith’s explanation and District Key Number Tables</a>). </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><em>Sidenote: Personally I have not come across these type of annotations very much, if at all, aside from the examples in Smith’s article. If you come across naturalization numbers in your research please send me a link to the list! </em></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Did your ancestor apply for citizenship in 1943 or later, and you’re wondering why you don’t see naturalization numbers on their passenger list? It’s probably because “the passenger lists were microfilmed in 1942/43, so records of immigrants who arrived earlier but did not begin the naturalization process until after 1942 could not be annotated” (Smith).  </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">These naturalization numbers should not be confused with Permit or Verification of Landing markings, which may also appear in the name column, and usually have a “P” or ‘V / L” before the set of numbers. Read more about these notations for immigrants wishing to travel in the page on JewishGen titled <a href="https://www.jewishgen.org/infofiles/manifests/name/" target="_blank">“Markings in the Manifest’s Name Column”</a> by Marian Smith. </p><h3 style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><br>DEP (Deportable / Deported) </h3>
