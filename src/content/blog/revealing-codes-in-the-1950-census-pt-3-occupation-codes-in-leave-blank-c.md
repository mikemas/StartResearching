<div class="sqs-html-content" data-sqsp-text-block-content>
  <p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Today we’re talking about the set of numbers in the column titled “LEAVE BLANK C” found on the far right of the 1950 US Census pages, under the heading “FOR PERSONS 14 YEARS OF AGE AND OLDER” and subheading “Class of worker”. </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">You’ll notice these numbers are three digits, three digits, then one digit, or XXX: XXX: X.  Each group of these numbers is translated as <strong>“occupation code: industry code: class code”</strong> which you’ll recognize as the same as the preceding three columns, Occupation (20c), Industry (20b), and Class of Worker (20c). </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Like the other leave blank columns, these numbers are not likely to tell us anything new. They are codes written after the census enumerator handed in their work and the editors began preparing them for the punch cards to be read by the tabulating machines. It can be helpful to us if the information in the preceding columns is hard to read or missing.</p>

<div class="sqs-html-content" data-sqsp-text-block-content>
  <p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">The 1955 Census Bureau document, <em>The 1950 Censuses - How They Were Taken </em>tells us “the occupation and industry coders used the manual, “Alphabetic Index of Occupations and Industries,” to determine the codes for the schedule entries. Occupations and industries not listed in the “Index” were coded by experienced persons until the regular coders became skilled in interpreting the entries. The verification procedure was the same as that for general coders except that 10 percent of the work of the qualified occupation and industry coders was verified.” (Chapter 6, page 3).</p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">We’ll use this same document to decipher our sets of numbers, and see if we can learn anything new.  I was able to find a copy through a quick online search, with a public file on the IPUMS USA website: “<a href="https://usa.ipums.org/usa/resources/volii/Occupations1950.pdf" target="_blank">Alphabetical Index of Occupations and Industries: 1950</a>”. Note link opens PDF. </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">So let’s take a closer look at our example selection of six individuals from an Augusta, Georgia census (<a href="https://1950census.archives.gov/search/?county=Augusta%2C%20Richmond&amp;page=1&amp;state=GA">ED 161-1; page 4</a>). Starting from the top, we have: </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Frank McGhee  - Labor - cottonseed oil co - P - <strong>970:469:1</strong> </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Odesa McGhee - Maid - Private Employ - P - <strong>720:826:1</strong></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Joe L. Byrd - Labor - Private Employ - P - <strong>970:826:1</strong></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Robert Golplin - Brickhacker - Brickyard - P - <strong>970:318:1</strong></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Dasey B. Holmiez  - Nurse - City Hospital - G - <strong>058:869:2</strong></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Robert B. Parker - Porter - 5 &amp; 10 (illegible word) - P - <strong>780:647:1</strong></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><em>(I may have butchered a name or two, but we’re going with “Dasey” here for now).</em> </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><br></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><span style="text-decoration:underline"><strong>Class Code</strong></span></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Let’s start with the last number of our set as it the easiest of the three sets to understand. This is the single digit at the end that most often appears as a “1” or “2” which is the <strong>class code</strong>, and is also shown in column 20c as either a letter “P” or letter “G”.  In the chart below, we learn “P” is for private employer, while “G” is for government. And if it was a “P” in column 20c, then the editor or tabulator would mark a “1” as the last digit of our code (or “2” for government, and so on). We also see other options for “O” and “NP” (translated as “3” and “4” respectively). </p>

<div class="sqs-html-content" data-sqsp-text-block-content>
  <p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">There is also a special classification, “5”, for those in armed forces. </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Using this information to review our example cases above, we have the majority “1”  and “P” for private employers, except for our Dasey that has a “2” as her final digit and a “G” for government. Her job is listed as a Nurse for the City Hospital - and knowing that she is a government employee may actually be new information (though it could also be a misclassification - take it as a lead on your investigation, not a fact). It also may change where you’ll find remaining hospital records. <br></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><span style="text-decoration:underline"><strong>Occupation</strong></span></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">The first set of three numbers, if you remember, is the <strong>occupation code</strong>.  You can look up these occupational codes and more starting on page 12 of our favorite document, the “<a href="https://usa.ipums.org/usa/resources/volii/Occupations1950.pdf" target="_blank">Alphabetical Index of Occupations and Industries: 1950</a>” (link opens PDF). It’s too long to post a copy of this section here - about five and a half pages. </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;"><span style="text-decoration:underline"><strong>Industry</strong></span></p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">The industry codes, or the middle three digits, start on page 18 of the document. There are 269 different occupation categories, summarized into 11 groups, as in the chart below. </p>

  <p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">So let’s take one last summary look at our six example individuals, and what their codes in “Leave Blank C” mean. </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Starting with the first line we have Frank McGhee, in “labor” at the Cottonseed Oil Co. For occupation code we have “970”, which simply means “labor, not otherwise classified”. Her industry code of “826” stands for “personal services: private households”.</p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Then we have Joe L. This all seems like a very vague description. </p><p class="" style="white-space:pre-wrap;">Next up we have another lodger, Robert Golpin, a “brickhacker” for a “brickyard”.
